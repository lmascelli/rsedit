NAME
    background - work that must not happen on the thread that draws

DESCRIPTION
    Anything that may take longer than a frame is a background job. Walking
    a directory tree for manual pages, searching a project, building an
    index: done where a command is done, each of those is the editor
    stopping. Done in the background and reported carelessly, each is a
    mode being changed underneath itself by another thread.

    A job is started by one call that names what to do and what to do when
    it is done.

        (background-call NAME JOB &optional ON-PROGRESS ON-DONE INTERVAL)
        (define-worker NAME FIBER &optional INTERVAL)
        (stop-worker NAME)
        (running-workers)
        (emit-event TOPIC)
        (completion-invalidate)

THE TWO SHAPES
    JOB is either a function of no arguments, called once, or a `fiber',
    resumed a step at a time. A fiber is the shape to use whenever the work
    divides, because each (yield) in it is a progress point and there is
    nowhere else progress could be reported from.

        (background-call 'manpage-index
                         (fiber (let ((left manpage-path))
                                  (while left
                                    (manpage--index-one (car left))
                                    (setq left (cdr left))
                                    (yield 'continue))))
                         'manpage--index-progress
                         'manpage--index-done)

    A `while' rather than a `dolist', and that is not a matter of taste: a
    (yield) is only legal where the evaluator can record a way back to it,
    which is a body statement or a form of a `while'. A `dolist' body is
    not one of those, and a yield there is refused -- so a fiber is
    written as a loop that walks its own list.

    `fiber' and `yield' are special forms of the interpreter rather than
    functions, so C-h f does not find them. This page is their
    documentation.

WHAT A YIELD ASKS FOR
    What a fiber yields says when it wants its next step:

        (yield)                      wait INTERVAL ms, 40 if not given
        (yield 'continue)            as soon as its turn comes round
        (yield '(sleep MS))          wait MS milliseconds
        (yield '(wait TOPIC...))     until one of the TOPICs is emitted
        (yield '(wait-at-most MS TOPIC...))
                                     the same, or MS milliseconds at most

    Any other value waits INTERVAL, as (yield) does, so a fiber written to
    yield its progress as a value goes on as it always did. A list that
    starts like one of these and is not one -- (sleep "soon") -- stops the
    job, with a line in the log saying what the shape is.

    The topics of a wait are the job's until a later wait names others, so
    (wait) and (wait-at-most MS) keep them. A wait for topics the job did
    not have yet costs one more step, a moment later: the step that named
    them may have looked just before one was emitted.

EVENTS
    A topic carries nothing. Emitting one says "go and look", and a job
    woken by it looks at whatever it was waiting to see change. So a burst
    of emissions is one step, not one each.

    The editor emits `buffer-changed' when the text of a buffer changes,
    and `syntax-changed' when a grammar or a syntax table does. A mode
    emits its own with `emit-event':

        (emit-event 'my-mode-refresh)
        (define-worker 'my-mode-indexer
          (fiber (while t
                   (my-mode-reindex)
                   (yield '(wait my-mode-refresh buffer-changed)))))

WHICH THREAD RUNS WHAT
    The job runs on the background thread. Both callbacks run on the thread
    that runs commands, between commands.

    That division is the whole of the arrangement. A job runs while you are
    typing: half-way through an insertion, inside a hook that is walking a
    list it is also changing. Anything it calls sees the editor in whatever
    state the command in progress has left it in, and no amount of care in
    the thing being called fixes that, because it is not the callee's
    fault.

    A callback queued to the command thread runs where a keystroke would
    run, with the same locks free and the same invariants holding. So a
    callback may do anything a command may do, and a job may not.

    The price is that a callback is not immediate. It is at most one pass
    of the event loop away -- the same delay a keystroke has.

THE RULE
    1.  Anything that may take longer than a frame is a background job, and
        a job is started by one call that names what to do and what to do
        when it is done.

    2.  A job never draws. It changes state; the renderer notices -- the
        end of every step tells it to look.

    3.  A job never assumes the editor stood still. The buffer may be gone,
        the variable may have been set again, the version it read may be
        two edits old. It re-checks before it stores, and what it cannot
        re-check it does not keep.

    4.  Progress is a signal, not a redraw. A job says there is more now;
        what more means is the mode's business.

    5.  The mode keeps itself consistent. Which is a fair thing to ask only
        because the signal arrives somewhere a mode can answer it.

    6.  Errors are logged, never raised. The command that started the job
        returned long ago; there is nobody to raise to.

    7.  A job is superseded, not duplicated. Starting one under a name that
        has one stops the old, and the old one's queued callbacks are
        dropped rather than delivered against the new one's state.

WHAT THE CALLBACKS ARE GIVEN
    The name the job ran under, and nothing else.

    Not what it produced, deliberately. Between the step that produced
    something and the callback that reports it, the buffer may have been
    killed and the variable set again -- so a callback that was handed a
    result would be handed a result about a world that no longer exists. A
    callback looks up what it needs, and finds either the current answer or
    nothing.

    ON-PROGRESS is called with NAME after every step but the last. ON-DONE
    is called once, with NAME and whether the job reached its end:

        reached its end                   (NAME t)
        an error, or a step that spent    (NAME nil)
          its allowance
        stopped, or replaced by a job     not called: whoever did it
          of the same name                knows already

    A job that failed still says so, because a mode that is never told
    stays waiting for ever.

SAYING THE CANDIDATES CHANGED
    The one signal common enough to be a primitive.

    A module whose completion candidates are built in the background calls
    `completion-invalidate' from its ON-PROGRESS. That forgets the answer
    remembered for the current input, so the next Tab asks again, and calls
    `*completion-invalidated-function*' if a presenter has registered one,
    so a list already on screen redraws with what there is now.

    Neither module has to have been loaded for the other to work, which is
    the point of it going through the editor rather than through a call
    from one to the other.

WHY THERE IS MORE THAN ONE THREAD
    One rule decides it:

        The shared thread may only run work that is guaranteed to return.

    A fiber's step is guaranteed to return, because `worker-fuel' cuts it
    off whether it wants to stop or not. A chunk of syntax colouring is
    the same. Any number of those share one thread and take turns, and a
    step costs nothing.

    Reading a child process's output is not guaranteed to return. Nor is
    walking a directory tree. These take as long as the system takes, and
    no budget shortens them -- so each gets a thread of its own, named after
    the buffer it writes into. `work-list' shows it, `stop-worker' with that
    name stops it, and closing the buffer stops it too.

    The rule is not decoration. `M-x compile' on a long build used to hold
    the shared thread for the whole build: colouring stopped, the file
    watcher stopped, the auto-saver stopped, and every worker and every
    background job stopped until the build finished. A second command
    started meanwhile did run -- a process begins the moment it is
    spawned -- but nothing read its output, so it printed into a pipe
    nobody was emptying and showed nothing until the build was over.

    So: if the work can be written as steps, write it as steps and it
    costs a fiber. If it blocks, it is a thread, and in Lisp that means
    `shell-command-start' -- which is the only door to blocking work that
    Lisp has, deliberately.

WHO GOES FIRST
    The shared thread runs two classes of job. Colouring and the
    prescanner are what you are waiting to see on the screen, and go
    first; everything else, every Lisp job among it, gets a step after
    every few of theirs -- `high-steps' of them, 4 unless set otherwise.
    So a busy worker cannot keep colour off the screen, and a large file
    being coloured cannot stop a worker for long.

WORKERS ARE THE OTHER HALF
    `define-worker' starts something that goes on for the life of the
    session: an index kept up to date, something that watches. A job
    finishes. They share a namespace on purpose -- with the editor's own
    work too -- so that `stop-worker' and `running-workers' mean one
    thing, and a name cannot be two.

    Each step of either gets `worker-fuel' to spend, separately from the
    budget commands run under: nothing the user does can cut a step short,
    and a step that runs past its allowance is stopped and the job with
    it, because a step that never ends holds the thread syntax colouring
    and the prescanner also run on.

WHAT IS RUNNING AND HOW IT IS TUNED
    `(work-list)' shows everything the scheduler runs, the editor's own
    work as well as Lisp's, one property list each: its name, its kind,
    its class, its state -- ready, running, sleeping or waiting -- and how
    many steps it has taken, in how long. `(running-workers)' is the part
    of it Lisp started.

    The scheduler's thresholds are options, read with `work-option' and
    set with `set-work-option', from the next step on -- in init.lisp as
    well. `(work-options)' lists them:

        high-steps      steps of colouring before another job's: 4
        event-delay     ms from an emission to the step it wakes: 20
        long-step       a step longer than this, in ms, is logged: 40
        late-high       colouring kept waiting this long is logged: 40
        late-normal     any other job kept waiting this long: 200
        stuck-step      a thread this slow to stop, in ms, is logged: 1000
        busy-share      a second busier than this percentage: 50
        summary-every   ms between two summaries of a report: 10000
        watchdog-every  ms between two looks of the watchdog: 250

    What they report goes to the log, in lines that start with [BKG].
