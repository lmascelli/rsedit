NAME
    background - work that goes on while you type

SYNOPSIS
    (background-call NAME JOB &optional ON-PROGRESS ON-DONE INTERVAL)
    (define-worker NAME FIBER &optional INTERVAL)
    (stop-worker NAME)
    (running-workers)
    (list-workers)
    (emit-event TOPIC)
    (work-list)
    (work-option NAME)
    (set-work-option NAME VALUE)
    (work-options)
    worker-fuel

DESCRIPTION
    A command runs while the editor waits for it: nothing is drawn and no
    key is read until it returns. Work that may take longer than that --
    walking a directory tree, indexing a project, anything that goes
    through something large -- is handed to the background instead, and
    goes on a step at a time while you type:

        (background-call 'my-index
                         (fiber (let ((left my-files))
                                  (while left
                                    (my-index-file (car left))
                                    (setq left (cdr left))
                                    (yield 'continue))))
                         'my-index-progress
                         'my-index-done)

    `background-call' runs a job that ends, and `define-worker' one that
    goes on for as long as the editor does: an index kept up to date as
    you edit, something that watches. Each has a name, and the name is
    how it is listed, stopped and replaced.

FIBERS
    A job is a function of no arguments, called once, or a fiber: a body
    that runs as far as a yield, and goes on from there at its next step.

        (fiber BODY...)
        (yield &optional WHEN)

    `fiber' and `yield' are part of the Lisp itself rather than functions,
    so C-h f does not find them, and this page is where they are described.

    A yield stands as a form of a body: the fiber's own, or that of a let,
    a progn, a while, an if, a when, a cond, or a function the fiber calls
    from one of those. As the argument of a call, as the value of a setq,
    or inside dolist, dotimes, mapc or funcall it is refused, and the job
    fails: so a fiber walks a list with while, as above.

    What a fiber yields says when its next step comes:

        (yield)                      after INTERVAL milliseconds, 40
                                     unless given
        (yield 'continue)            as soon as its turn comes round
        (yield '(sleep MS))          after MS milliseconds
        (yield '(wait TOPIC...))     when one of the TOPICs is emitted
        (yield '(wait-at-most MS TOPIC...))
                                     the same, or after MS at most

    Anything else waits INTERVAL, as (yield) does, but a list that starts
    like one of these and is not one, such as (sleep "soon"), makes the
    job fail. A wait keeps the topics it names until a later one names
    others, so (wait) waits for the same ones again.

EVENTS
    A topic is a name, and emitting one carries nothing else: it says "go
    and look", and the job it wakes looks for itself at what it was
    waiting to see change. Emissions that come together wake a job once.
    The editor emits buffer-changed when the text of a buffer changes, and
    syntax-changed when a mode's colouring or syntax table does; a module
    emits its own with `emit-event':

        (define-worker 'my-indexer
          (fiber (while t
                   (my-reindex)
                   (yield '(wait my-refresh buffer-changed)))))
        (emit-event 'my-refresh)

    The step an emission wakes a job for comes event-delay milliseconds
    after the first emission the job has not seen. A wait that names
    topics the job was not waiting for yet takes one more step, a moment
    later, in case one of them was emitted just before.

WHERE THE CALLBACKS RUN
    A job runs while you type, and whatever it looks at is as the command
    in progress has left it. ON-PROGRESS and ON-DONE run between commands,
    as the command of a key does, and may do whatever a command may: show
    a message, change a buffer, draw a list again. So a job computes and
    stores, and its callbacks show what it found.

    ON-PROGRESS is called with NAME after every step but the last, so each
    yield of a fiber is a point of progress, and a plain function has
    none. ON-DONE is called once, with NAME and what became of the job:

        it reached its end                          t
        it failed: an error, a wrong yield, or      nil
          a step that spent all its worker-fuel
        it was stopped, or replaced by another      ON-DONE is not called
          of the same name

    Neither callback is given what the job made, because by the time it
    runs the buffer the job was for may have gone, or the job may have been
    started again. The job stores what it makes where the callback can
    look, and the callback looks. A variable the job sets has to have a
    value before the job starts, from a `defvar': in this Lisp a `setq' of
    a name with no value makes a variable of the function doing it, and
    the variable goes, with what the job stored in it, when the function
    returns.

    A job that finds completion candidates says it has more from its
    ON-PROGRESS, with `completion-invalidate', so that a list of them on
    the screen is drawn again: the `completion' page has it.

NAMES
    A name is a symbol or a string, and one name means one thing: the
    workers, the jobs and the editor's own work share them. Starting work
    under a name in use replaces what ran under it: the old work stops
    before its next step, and the callbacks it had queued are dropped. That
    is what makes loading a module twice, or pressing a key twice, start
    one job and not two.

    `stop-worker' stops the work called NAME, whatever it is, at the end of
    the step it is taking, and its ON-DONE is not called; a shell command
    is stopped so too, by the name of its buffer. The editor's own work
    stops just the same, and stays stopped until the editor starts again:
    stopping highlighter stops the colouring. `running-workers' lists the
    work started from Lisp that is still running, and M-x list-workers
    says it in the echo area.

WHAT IS RUNNING
    (work-list) lists everything in the background, the editor's own work
    as well as Lisp's, one property list each:

        (name "highlighter" kind job class high state waiting
         steps 1204 total-ms 412 max-ms 38)

    A job's class is high for what you are waiting to see, the colouring
    and the prescanner, and normal for the rest. Its state is ready,
    running, sleeping or waiting; steps says how many steps it has taken,
    total-ms how long they took together and max-ms how long the longest
    did. The figures are brought up to date when the scheduler runs out of
    work, and every tenth of a second while it is busy. A shell command is
    a thread of its own, with kind thread, a state of running or stopping,
    and running-ms, how long it has been running.

WHO GOES FIRST
    The jobs take turns on a thread of their own. The colouring and the
    prescanner go first, and the other jobs, every Lisp job among them,
    get a step after every high-steps of theirs: 4 unless set. So a busy
    worker cannot keep colour off the screen, and a large file being
    coloured cannot hold a worker back for long.

    A step has worker-fuel to spend, 200000 unless set: how much Lisp it
    may evaluate, apart from what commands are given. A step that spends
    it all is stopped, and its job fails, because a step that never yields
    would hold the colouring up for ever. A job that needs more yields
    more often, or is started again after a `setq' of worker-fuel: the
    value is read when a job starts.

TUNING THE SCHEDULER
    The scheduler's thresholds are options, read with `work-option' and
    set with `set-work-option', from init.lisp as well; a change counts
    from the scheduler's next step. `work-options' lists them with their
    values:

        high-steps      steps of colouring before another job's: 4
        event-delay     ms from an emission to the step it wakes: 20
        long-step       a step longer than this, in ms, is logged: 40
        late-high       colouring kept waiting this long, in ms, is
                        logged: 40
        late-normal     any other job kept waiting this long: 200
        stuck-step      a step still running after this many ms, or a
                        thread this slow to stop, is logged: 1000
        busy-share      a second busier than this percentage is logged:
                        50
        summary-every   ms between two summaries of one report: 10000
        watchdog-every  ms between two looks of the watchdog: 250

    A value an option does not take is refused, and the error says which
    it takes. What these report goes to the log, in lines that start with
    [BKG].

WHEN A STEP DOES NOT END
    A step that does not end cannot say so, since the thread that would
    say it is the one running it. So a thread of its own looks from
    outside, every watchdog-every, and writes what it sees in the log:

        [BKG] stuck step: manpage-index running for 1.0 s

    at stuck-step, and again each time the step has run twice as long. A
    job that fails says why in the log too, with its name:

        [BKG] error: my-index: WrongArgumentType { expected: "List", got: 5 }

    The `diagnostics' page says where the log is.

EXAMPLES
    A sum worked out in the background, said when it is ready:

        (defvar my-sum nil)
        (defun my-done (name ok) (message "%s finished: %s" name ok))
        (background-call 'my-sum (lambda () (setq my-sum (my-slow-sum)))
                         nil 'my-done)

    A worker that does something every second:

        (define-worker 'my-ticker
          (fiber (while t (my-tick) (yield '(sleep 1000)))))

    And stopping it:

        (stop-worker 'my-ticker)

    Colouring first for six steps in a row rather than four:

        (set-work-option 'high-steps 6)

    More for each step of the jobs started from now on:

        (setq worker-fuel 400000)

    What is running, and how long its steps take:

        (work-list)
