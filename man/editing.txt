DESCRIPTION
    The editor's commands are built out of two halves: a unit of text and
    something to do to it. Learn the units and the rest follows, because
    the same letter means the same thing wherever it appears.

        f  b            forward, backward
        a  e            start, end
        d  k            delete, kill
        Control         a character, or a line
        Meta            a word, or a paragraph
        Control-Meta    a balanced expression

    So C-f is a character forward, M-f a word forward, C-M-f an expression
    forward. C-d deletes a character, M-d kills a word, C-M-k kills an
    expression, and C-k kills to the end of the line.

    Killing is cutting, and what you kill can be put back: the `killing'
    page is about that, and about the region. The `indentation' page is
    about Tab and Return.

MOVING
        C-f  C-b        a character
        M-f  M-b        a word
        C-n  C-p        a line, keeping the column
        C-a  C-e        to the start or the end of the line; also Home, End
        M-}  M-{        a paragraph
        M-<  M->        to the start or the end of the buffer; also C-Home,
                        C-End
        C-M-f  C-M-b    a balanced expression
        M-g g           to a line, by number

    A word is a run of letters, digits and underscores. A paragraph ends
    at a blank line. A balanced expression is a list, a string or an atom
    such as a name or a number, with any prefix in front of it, such as the
    quote in 'foo.

    What counts as a bracket, a string or a comment comes from the mode:
    `set-syntax-pairs' and `set-comment-syntax' in the `modes' page. A mode
    that says nothing still gets the three kinds of bracket, double quotes
    and backslash. A bracket inside a string or a comment is text, so C-M-f
    steps over (message "a )") in one move. A comment, of either kind, is
    passed over as a space is: C-M-f goes over it and the expression after
    it. From inside a name, C-M-f goes to its end.

    C-M-f stops at the end of the list it is in, and does not move when the
    list ends before the count does; C-M-k there kills nothing. C-M-u goes
    up out of the list, to its opening bracket, and C-M-d down into the next
    list inside it. M-x up-list goes up and out the other way, past the
    closing bracket.

SEEING WHICH BRACKETS PAIR
    In the window you are in, the bracket next to the cursor is drawn with
    the one that pairs with it: an opening bracket just after the cursor,
    or else a closing one just before it, so that typing ) shows at once
    which ( it closes. Both are drawn in the face show-paren-match. A pair
    of two kinds, such as ( closed by ], and a bracket that nothing pairs
    with, are drawn in show-paren-mismatch; the `themes' page says how to
    change either face.

    Brackets are read as C-M-f reads them: one in a string or a comment
    is text, and is paired with nothing.

    The drawing is the minor mode `show-paren-mode', on in every buffer:
    M-x show-paren-mode turns it off in the buffer you are in, and on
    again. The `modes' page says how to keep a minor mode off in the
    buffers of one major mode, or of every one.

    The pair is looked for up to 100 kilobytes of text away from the
    cursor; a bracket whose partner is further than that is not drawn at
    all. A closing bracket under a block cursor, as in Vim's normal state,
    is after point, not before it, and is paired once point is past it.

COUNTS
    A number before a command means "do it that many times": C-u 20 C-f
    goes forward twenty characters, and C-u 3 M-d kills three words. C-u
    on its own means four, and C-u C-u sixteen. A count reaches the
    commands that take one: the motions by character, word, line,
    paragraph and expression, the kills by word and by expression, C-d,
    C-o, C-x e, and a character you type: C-u 3 x types xxx. The others
    happen once.

    For the motions and the deletions a negative count goes the other way:
    C-u - 2 C-n goes up two lines, as (next-line -2) does, and C-u - M-d
    kills the word before point. Zero goes nowhere.

TYPING
    A character you type inserts itself. A run of typing undoes a word or
    two at a time, not a character at a time.

    Backspace deletes the character behind point and C-d the one in
    front. Deleting does not save what it deletes; killing does.

PAIRS
    Typing an opening bracket inserts the closing one too, with point
    between them, and typing the closing bracket then steps over the one
    already there. Deleting either half of an empty pair, with Backspace
    or C-d, deletes both; a pair with only spaces or tabs inside counts as
    empty, and one with anything else inside loses only the half deleted.
    A double quote pairs the same way, and typing the quote that ends a
    string steps over the one already there.

    No partner is added in front of a word, since typing ( before foo is
    about to wrap it; nor when the list is already closed further on; nor
    inside a comment or a string. A closing bracket typed where the list
    is closed further on goes to that bracket rather than adding one.
    Pasted text is never paired.

    The pairs are the mode's, the same ones C-M-f follows. Pairing is the
    minor mode `electric-pair-mode', on in the buffers of fundamental-mode
    and rust-mode; `electric-pair-enable' turns it on in the buffers of
    another mode, those open and those to come. In rust-mode quotes are not
    paired, since 'a is a lifetime there. M-x electric-pair-mode turns all
    of it off in the buffer you are in, and on again, and setting
    `electric-pair-skip-self' to nil stops only the stepping over.

    Return between the halves of an empty pair, as in {|}, opens a line
    between them for point and puts the closer on the line after, as in
    Emacs; with `electric-indent-mode' on, both lines are indented, so
    typing a block's body starts where it belongs:

        fn main() {
            |
        }

    Setting `electric-pair-open-newline-between-pairs' to nil leaves
    Return a plain newline there.

LINES
        C-o             open a blank line above this one
        C-x C-o         delete the blank lines around here
        M-^             join this line to the one above
        C-x C-t         swap this line with the one above

    C-o and M-x open-line-below indent the new line as Tab would. C-x C-o
    leaves one blank line in a run of them, deletes a blank line on its
    own, and on a line with text deletes the blank lines after it. M-^
    leaves one space at the join, or none after an opening bracket or
    before a closing one. M-x duplicate-line puts a copy of the line below
    it.

CASE AND SPACE
        M-u  M-l        upcase, downcase the word in front
        M-c             capitalise it
        M-<space>       leave exactly one space here
        M-\             delete the spaces and tabs around here

    With a region, M-u, M-l and M-c change the whole region instead. Both
    space commands stay on one line, so they never join two.

SPACES AT THE ENDS OF LINES
    Spaces and Tabs left at the end of a line are drawn in the face
    trailing-whitespace, on a red background unless a theme says
    otherwise, so that they can be seen and taken away; a line of nothing
    but blanks is drawn whole. Only buffers visiting a file draw them:
    listings, help and the output of commands do not. On the line you are
    typing on, the blanks the cursor is among, or at the end of, are not
    drawn: a space just typed before the next word is not a mistake. In
    another window showing the same buffer they are.

    The drawing is the minor mode `whitespace-mode', on in every buffer:
    M-x whitespace-mode turns it off in the buffer you are in, and on
    again. In a buffer visiting no file the mode can be on, and draws
    nothing. The `modes' page says how to keep a minor mode off in the
    buffers of one major mode, or of every one.

    M-x delete-trailing-whitespace takes them away: on the lines of the
    region when it is active, and otherwise in the whole buffer, where it
    also takes away the blank lines at the end, all but the newline after
    the last line of text, unless `delete-trailing-lines' is nil. The
    cursor stays where it was, or goes to the end of its line when it was
    among the blanks; the echo area says how many lines were cleaned, and
    one C-/ puts them all back. From Lisp, START and END name the lines to
    clean instead of the region.

    In a buffer with the minor mode `whitespace-cleanup-mode' on, every
    save cleans the whole buffer that way just before it is written:
    C-x C-s, C-x C-w, and the saves C-x C-c and save-some-buffers offer.
    The mode is off until you turn it on: M-x whitespace-cleanup-mode
    does, in the buffer you are in, and the status line then shows Clean;
    `minor-mode-enable' does in the buffers of a major mode, or of every
    one. Leave it off where the blanks mean something: Markdown ends a
    line with two spaces to break it. The copies auto-save makes are
    never cleaned.

TRANSPOSING
        C-t             swap the characters either side of point
        M-t             swap the words either side of point
        C-x C-t         swap this line with the one above

    C-t and M-t leave point after both, so pressing again carries the same
    character or word on, one more step forward. C-x C-t moves this line
    up and point with it, so pressing again moves it up again. At the end
    of a line C-t swaps the two characters before point, which fixes a typo
    you have just made.

ZAPPING
    M-z and a character kills everything up to and including the next one
    of that character: M-z ) kills to the end of a call. C-y puts it back.

COMMENTS
    M-; comments out the region, or the line when there is no region, and
    takes the comments off again when every line with text on it already
    has one. The comment opener goes at the indentation of the least
    indented line, so the block keeps its shape. C-x C-; does the line
    point is on, whatever is selected.

    How a comment is written comes from the mode, from
    `set-comment-syntax'. In a language with only block comments, M-; and
    C-x C-; wrap the lines in one. A mode that has said nothing gets a
    message rather than a guess.

    M-x comment-indent starts a comment at the end of the line, beside the
    code, or goes into the one already there. `comment-region' and
    `uncomment-region' go one way only, without deciding: they and
    comment-indent need a mode with line comments.

UNDOING
        C-/             undo, also C-_ and C-x u
        C-?             redo
        C-g             give up what is half done

    Undo takes back one command at a time, and moves point to where the
    change was. After an undo a plain u undoes again, and after a redo a
    plain r redoes again. A new edit after an undo forgets what could have
    been redone. Some terminals cannot send C-?: there, use M-x redo, or
    bind it to a key that arrives. Like any edit, an undo ends the region,
    and what is drawn over the text, highlights and virtual text, moves
    with it; a read-only buffer refuses it.

    The history keeps up to a mebibyte of deleted text in each buffer, and
    lets the oldest changes go past that; M-x set-undo-limit changes it.
    Code that makes several edits can split them into steps with
    `undo-boundary'.

    C-g abandons a key sequence typed halfway, a count, and the region. It
    does not stop a command that is already running.

WATCHING EDITS
    `track-changes-begin' starts watching the edits to the buffer, and
    `track-changes-end' stops and says what they changed, as
    (START REMOVED INSERTED): from START, REMOVED characters became the
    string INSERTED, or nil when nothing was edited. It keeps the text the
    edits reached, not a copy of the buffer, so it costs what was typed,
    not what the file is. A word typed and deleted again is no change but
    where it was. An undo counts as an edit; one that reaches outside what
    was edited makes all of it the change. vim watches an Insert session
    this way, for what . repeats.

REPEATING
    C-x z repeats the last command, and pressing z again repeats it again.
    For anything longer, record what you did and play it back: see the
    `macros' page.

EXAMPLES
    Insert text from Lisp, several pieces at once:

        (insert "total: " 42 "\n")

    The line point is on, and its number:

        (list (line-number-at-point) (current-line))

    Do something at the end of the buffer and come back:

        (let ((here (point)))
          (goto-char (point-max))
          (insert "\n;; end\n")
          (goto-char here))

    Pair brackets in C as well, but not quotes:

        (electric-pair-enable 'cpp-mode)
        (put 'cpp-mode 'electric-pair-inhibit-quotes t)

    Keep every closing bracket you type, even next to one already there:

        (setq electric-pair-skip-self nil)

    Stop drawing the bracket paired with the one at the cursor, in the
    buffers opened from now on:

        (minor-mode-disable 'show-paren-mode)

    Leave the spaces at the ends of lines undrawn in one mode, where they
    mean something:

        (defun my-markdown-setup () (minor-mode-off 'whitespace-mode))
        (add-hook 'markdown-mode "after-change-major-mode-hook"
                  'my-markdown-setup)

    Clean every file of them as it is saved, but leave the blank lines at
    the end alone:

        (minor-mode-enable 'whitespace-cleanup-mode)
        (setq delete-trailing-lines nil)

    Clean only the Rust files:

        (minor-mode-enable 'whitespace-cleanup-mode 'rust-mode)

    Give the commands that have no key one of yours:

        (define-key nil "C-c d" 'duplicate-line)
        (define-key nil "C-c ;" 'comment-indent)

    What some edits changed, without copying the buffer:

        (track-changes-begin)
        (insert "abc")
        (track-changes-end)       ; (START 0 "abc")

    Comment out a whole buffer from Lisp:

        (comment-region (point-min) (point-max))
