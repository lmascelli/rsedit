DESCRIPTION
    The editor's commands are built out of two halves: a unit of text and
    something to do to it. Learn the units and the rest follows, because
    the same letter means the same thing wherever it appears.

        f  b            forward, backward
        a  e            start, end
        d  k            delete, kill
        Control         a character, or a line
        Meta            a word, or a paragraph
        Control-Meta    a balanced expression

    So C-f is a character forward, M-f a word forward, C-M-f an expression
    forward. C-d deletes a character, M-d kills a word, C-M-k kills an
    expression, and C-k kills to the end of the line.

    Killing is cutting, and what you kill can be put back: the `killing'
    page is about that, and about the region. The `indentation' page is
    about Tab and Return.

MOVING
        C-f  C-b        a character
        M-f  M-b        a word
        C-n  C-p        a line, keeping the column
        C-a  C-e        to the start or the end of the line; also Home, End
        M-}  M-{        a paragraph
        M-<  M->        to the start or the end of the buffer; also C-Home,
                        C-End
        C-M-f  C-M-b    a balanced expression
        M-g g           to a line, by number

    The arrows, C-n, C-p, C-a, C-e, Home and End run the move- commands:
    move-next-line, move-previous-line, move-beginning-of-line and
    move-end-of-line. Where the buffer wraps its long lines, with
    visual-line-mode, they move by the rows on the screen; elsewhere by
    lines. The Lisp commands next-line, previous-line, beginning-of-line
    and end-of-line always move by lines, whatever the buffer shows: code
    that works on a file's lines keeps using them. The `wrapping' page says
    more. A mode that binds those keys for something else keeps them: at a
    prompt the arrows, C-p and C-n go through the history, and in a list of
    candidates they choose one.

    A word is a run of letters, digits and underscores. A paragraph ends
    at a blank line. A balanced expression is a list, a string or an atom
    such as a name or a number, with any prefix in front of it, such as the
    quote in 'foo.

    What counts as a bracket, a string or a comment comes from the mode:
    `set-syntax-pairs' and `set-comment-syntax' in the `modes' page. A mode
    that says nothing still gets the three kinds of bracket, double quotes
    and backslash. A bracket inside a string or a comment is text, so C-M-f
    steps over (message "a )") in one move. A comment, of either kind, is
    passed over as a space is: C-M-f goes over it and the expression after
    it. From inside a name, C-M-f goes to its end.

    C-M-f stops at the end of the list it is in, and does not move when the
    list ends before the count does; C-M-k there kills nothing. C-M-u goes
    up out of the list, to its opening bracket, and C-M-d down into the next
    list inside it. M-x up-list goes up and out the other way, past the
    closing bracket.

IDENTIFIER PARTS
    M-x subword-mode makes M-f and M-b move by parts of an identifier, and
    M-d and M-Backspace kill the same parts. It is a minor mode, off until
    you turn it on in a buffer; the status line then shows Subword.
    Counts, including zero and negative counts, work as for whole words.

    Parts meet where lower case becomes upper case, before the last
    capital of an acronym followed by lower case, and between letters and
    digits: camel|Case, HTTP|Server, item|42. Positions count Unicode
    characters. Casing and transposing commands still use whole words.

    `subword-separators' is a string of literal characters, initially "_":
    snake_case has two parts. A hyphen stays inside an identifier unless
    you include it. Other punctuation and whitespace separate words as
    usual. For both snake_case and dash-case:

        (require 'subword)
        (setq subword-separators "_-")
        (subword-mode 1)

    For a different choice in just the current buffer:

        (buffer-put 'subword-separators "_-:")

    A string in that buffer property overrides the global value; nil
    removes the override. An empty string keeps underscores and hyphens
    inside identifiers. Runs of separators are crossed on the way to the
    next part, as spaces are with ordinary word movement and killing.

SEEING THE CURRENT LINE
    M-x hl-line-mode highlights the whole logical line containing point,
    across the width of the text area, including padding after the text.
    With visual-line-mode on, all its visible wrapped rows are highlighted.
    Only the selected window draws it; the minibuffer, line-number gutter
    and status line do not. The mode is off initially and per buffer; the
    status line shows HL when it is on. M-x global-hl-line-mode turns it
    on in every buffer, those open and those to come, and off again.

    The face hl-line supplies a bright-black background by default, and
    each theme that ships gives it a colour of its own, between the page's
    and the region's; monochrome, which has no colours, draws the line in
    bold. Syntax colours remain visible; the region, search matches and other
    explicit backgrounds can cover it. The `themes' page explains faces.
    For example, to choose a different background:

        (require 'hl-line)
        (set-face 'hl-line nil "#303030")
        (hl-line-mode 1)

SEEING WHICH BRACKETS PAIR
    In the window you are in, the bracket next to the cursor is drawn with
    the one that pairs with it: an opening bracket just after the cursor,
    or else a closing one just before it, so that typing ) shows at once
    which ( it closes. Both are drawn in the face show-paren-match. A pair
    of two kinds, such as ( closed by ], and a bracket that nothing pairs
    with, are drawn in show-paren-mismatch; the `themes' page says how to
    change either face.

    Brackets are read as C-M-f reads them: one in a string or a comment
    is text, and is paired with nothing.

    The drawing is the minor mode `show-paren-mode', on in every buffer
    whose role admits it -- those you edit, and the prompts: M-x
    show-paren-mode turns it off in the buffer you are in, and on again, and
    M-x global-show-paren-mode off in every buffer, those open and those to
    come, and on again. The `modes' page says how to keep a minor mode off in
    the buffers of one major mode, or of every one.

    The pair is looked for up to 100 kilobytes of text away from the
    cursor; a bracket whose partner is further than that is not drawn at
    all.

    Where the cursor stands on a character, as in vim's Normal state, the
    bracket under it is the one drawn, a closing one as well as an opening
    one, and a closing bracket just before it is not: the cursor covers the
    character after point. A buffer asks for that with its property
    show-paren-under-cursor, which vim sets in Normal state and clears in
    the others, as the `vim' page says; any buffer can ask the same:

        (buffer-put 'show-paren-under-cursor t)

COUNTS
    A number before a command means "do it that many times": C-u 20 C-f
    goes forward twenty characters, and C-u 3 M-d kills three words. C-u
    on its own means four, and C-u C-u sixteen. A count reaches the
    commands that take one: the motions by character, word, line,
    paragraph and expression, the kills by word and by expression, C-d,
    C-o, C-x e, and a character you type: C-u 3 x types xxx. The others
    happen once.

    For the motions and the deletions a negative count goes the other way:
    C-u - 2 C-n goes up two lines, as (next-line -2) does, and C-u - M-d
    kills the word before point. Zero goes nowhere.

    C-u starts a count only before a command's keys: after C-c it is the
    second key of C-c C-u, as a mode may bind it.

TYPING
    A character you type inserts itself. A run of typing undoes a word or
    two at a time, not a character at a time.

    Backspace deletes the character behind point and C-d the one in
    front. Deleting does not save what it deletes; killing does.

    Insert turns overwriting on, and off again. While it is on the status
    line shows Ovwrt, and a character you type takes the place of the one
    after point instead of pushing it along; at the end of a line it is
    added, so that a line never eats the one below. Return, a paste and
    what Lisp inserts go in as ever. Overwriting is the minor mode
    `overwrite-mode', on in the one buffer where you turned it on.

PAIRS
    Typing an opening bracket inserts the closing one too, with point
    between them, and typing the closing bracket then steps over the one
    already there. Deleting either half of an empty pair, with Backspace
    or C-d, deletes both; a pair with only spaces or tabs inside counts as
    empty, and one with anything else inside loses only the half deleted.
    A double quote pairs the same way, and typing the quote that ends a
    string steps over the one already there.

    No partner is added in front of a word, since typing ( before foo is
    about to wrap it; nor when the list is already closed further on; nor
    inside a comment or a string. A closing bracket typed where the list
    is closed further on goes to that bracket rather than adding one.
    Pasted text is never paired.

    The pairs are the mode's, the same ones C-M-f follows. Pairing is the
    minor mode `electric-pair-mode', on in the buffers of fundamental-mode
    and rust-mode; `electric-pair-enable' turns it on in the buffers of
    another mode, those open and those to come. In rust-mode quotes are not
    paired, since 'a is a lifetime there. M-x electric-pair-mode turns all
    of it off in the buffer you are in, and on again, and setting
    `electric-pair-skip-self' to nil stops only the stepping over. M-x
    global-electric-pair-mode turns it on in every buffer, whatever its
    mode, and off in all of them; the buffers of fundamental-mode and
    rust-mode made after that still get it.

    Return between the halves of an empty pair, as in {|}, opens a line
    between them for point and puts the closer on the line after, as in
    Emacs; with `electric-indent-mode' on, both lines are indented, so
    typing a block's body starts where it belongs:

        fn main() {
            |
        }

    Setting `electric-pair-open-newline-between-pairs' to nil leaves
    Return a plain newline there.

LINES
        C-o             open a blank line above this one
        C-x C-o         delete the blank lines around here
        M-^             join this line to the one above
        C-x C-t         swap this line with the one above

    C-o and M-x open-line-below indent the new line as Tab would. C-x C-o
    leaves one blank line in a run of them, deletes a blank line on its
    own, and on a line with text deletes the blank lines after it. M-^
    leaves one space at the join, or none after an opening bracket or
    before a closing one. M-x duplicate-line puts a copy of the line below
    it.

CASE AND SPACE
        M-u  M-l        upcase, downcase the word in front
        M-c             capitalise it
        M-<space>       leave exactly one space here
        M-\             delete the spaces and tabs around here

    With a region, M-u, M-l and M-c change the whole region instead. Both
    space commands stay on one line, so they never join two.

SPACES AT THE ENDS OF LINES
    Spaces and Tabs left at the end of a line are drawn in the face
    trailing-whitespace, on a red background unless a theme says
    otherwise, so that they can be seen and taken away; a line of nothing
    but blanks is drawn whole. Only buffers visiting a file draw them:
    listings, help and the output of commands do not. On the line you are
    typing on, the blanks the cursor is among, or at the end of, are not
    drawn: a space just typed before the next word is not a mistake. In
    another window showing the same buffer they are.

    The drawing is the minor mode `whitespace-mode', on in every buffer: M-x
    whitespace-mode turns it off in the buffer you are in, and on again, and
    M-x global-whitespace-mode off in every buffer, those open and those to
    come, and on again. In a buffer visiting no file the mode can be on, and
    draws nothing. The `modes' page says how to keep a minor mode off in the
    buffers of one major mode, or of every one.

    M-x delete-trailing-whitespace takes them away: on the lines of the
    region when it is active, and otherwise in the whole buffer, where it
    also takes away the blank lines at the end, all but the newline after
    the last line of text, unless `delete-trailing-lines' is nil. The
    cursor stays where it was, or goes to the end of its line when it was
    among the blanks; the echo area says how many lines were cleaned, and
    one C-/ puts them all back. From Lisp, START and END name the lines to
    clean instead of the region.

    In a buffer with the minor mode `whitespace-cleanup-mode' on, every
    save cleans the whole buffer through its `before-save-hook', even
    with an active region, just before the text to write is copied:
    C-x C-s, C-x C-w, and the saves C-x C-c and save-some-buffers offer.
    The mode is off until you turn it on: M-x whitespace-cleanup-mode
    does, in the buffer you are in, and the status line then shows Clean;
    `minor-mode-enable' does in the buffers of a major mode, or of every
    one. Leave it off where the blanks mean something: Markdown ends a
    line with two spaces to break it. The copies auto-save makes are
    never cleaned.

TRANSPOSING
        C-t             swap the characters either side of point
        M-t             swap the words either side of point
        C-x C-t         swap this line with the one above

    C-t and M-t leave point after both, so pressing again carries the same
    character or word on, one more step forward. C-x C-t moves this line
    up and point with it, so pressing again moves it up again. At the end
    of a line C-t swaps the two characters before point, which fixes a typo
    you have just made.

ZAPPING
    M-z and a character kills everything up to and including the next one
    of that character: M-z ) kills to the end of a call. C-y puts it back.

COMMENTS
    M-; comments out the region, or the line when there is no region, and
    takes the comments off again when every line with text on it already
    has one. The comment opener goes at the indentation of the least
    indented line, so the block keeps its shape. C-x C-; does the line
    point is on, whatever is selected.

    How a comment is written comes from the mode, from
    `set-comment-syntax'. In a language with only block comments, M-; and
    C-x C-; wrap the lines in one. A mode that has said nothing gets a
    message rather than a guess.

    M-x comment-indent starts a comment at the end of the line, beside the
    code, or goes into the one already there. `comment-region' and
    `uncomment-region' go one way only, without deciding: they and
    comment-indent need a mode with line comments.

UNDOING
        C-/             undo, also C-_ and C-x u
        C-?             redo
        C-g             give up what is half done

    Undo takes back one command at a time, and moves point to where the
    change was. After an undo a plain u undoes again, and after a redo a
    plain r redoes again. A new edit after an undo forgets what could have
    been redone. Some terminals cannot send C-?: there, use M-x redo, or
    bind it to a key that arrives. Like any edit, an undo ends the region,
    and what is drawn over the text, highlights and virtual text, moves
    with it; a read-only buffer refuses it.

    The history keeps up to a mebibyte of deleted text in each buffer, and
    lets the oldest changes go past that; M-x set-undo-limit changes it.
    Code that makes several edits can split them into steps with
    `undo-boundary'.

    C-g abandons a key sequence typed halfway, a count, and the region. It
    does not stop a command that is already running.

SEVERAL REPLACEMENTS AT ONCE
    `replace-regions' takes a list of (START END TEXT) and puts each TEXT
    in place of the text from START to END. Every offset is read in the
    buffer as it is before the first replacement, so a list worked out
    against the text can be handed over as it is, in any order: there is
    no need to correct the later offsets for the earlier changes.

    Point stays where it was in the text. Before a replacement, or on its
    start, it does not move; inside one it goes to the start; at its end
    or after, it moves with the text, so it stays on the same character.
    An insertion, START equal to END, at point leaves point after the new
    text. Insertions at one place come out in the order given, and before
    a replacement that starts there.

    The whole list is one step of undo. Two replacements that overlap, one
    that reaches outside the buffer, or a read-only buffer are an error,
    and then nothing has changed. Two that only touch are fine.

GOING AND COMING BACK
    `save-excursion' runs its body and then puts point back where it was,
    however the body ends, an error included, and answers what the body
    answers. It is for code that goes somewhere else to read or change
    the text, and should leave the cursor where the person typing left
    it:

        (save-excursion
          (goto-char (point-min))
          (insert ";; Generated, do not edit.\n"))

    Point is kept as a marker, as the `markers' page describes, so it
    moves with the text the body edits. Text inserted or deleted before it
    carries it along, and it comes back to the same character, not to the
    same number: above, it comes back after the new line, on the text it
    was on. Text inserted exactly at point goes after it. Inside a span
    `replace-regions' replaces, it goes to the start of the span, as point
    itself does.

    Only point is put back, in the buffer that was current. A buffer the
    body switches to stays the one shown, and the mark stays where the
    body left it. Emacs puts the current buffer back as well; here a
    buffer is current for good only when it is shown, and putting the old
    one back would undo what the body showed. In a buffer the body kills
    or renames, nothing is put back.

WATCHING EDITS
    `track-changes-begin' starts watching the edits to the buffer, and
    `track-changes-end' stops and says what they changed, as
    (START REMOVED INSERTED REMOVED-TEXT): from START, the REMOVED
    characters REMOVED-TEXT became the string INSERTED, or nil when
    nothing was edited. It keeps the text the edits reached, not a copy of
    the buffer, so it costs what was typed, not what the file is. A word
    typed and deleted again is no change but where it was. An undo counts
    as an edit; one that reaches outside what was edited makes all of it
    the change, and then REMOVED-TEXT is nil, because what it went over is
    no longer known. So is it after the file is read again.

    Several can watch the same buffer, each under its own KEY, a symbol
    given to both calls: beginning again under a KEY forgets only what
    that KEY watched, and ending it leaves the others watching. Without a
    KEY, or with nil, the watcher is the one that gives none. vim watches
    an Insert session that way, for what . repeats, so a KEY of yours
    leaves it alone.

COLUMNS IN OTHER UNITS
    A position counts characters, and so does a column. A program outside
    the editor may count a column another way: in UTF-8 bytes, or in UTF-16
    code units, where a character outside the Basic Multilingual Plane,
    such as most emoji, takes two. `position-to-line-column' turns a
    position into a line and a column counted in the unit you name, and
    `line-column-to-position' turns them back. The unit is char, utf-16 or
    utf-8; without one, a column counts characters, as `current-column'
    does. Lines count from 1, as `goto-line' does, and columns from 0.

    On the line "è😀x", x is at column 2 in characters, 3 in UTF-16 and 6
    in UTF-8. A column past the end of its line is the line's end; one
    that falls inside a character, as column 2 in UTF-16 falls inside 😀,
    is the position before that character; a line past the last is the end
    of the buffer. A line ends at a newline only, so a carriage return
    before it is a character of the line, and is counted. Each conversion
    reads only the line it is on, however long the buffer is.

    `string-line-column' measures a string the same way, before it is in a
    buffer or after it has left one: it returns how many newlines the
    string holds and the column of its end, counted from its last newline,
    or from its start when it has none. Put at line L and column C, the
    string ends at line L plus its newlines; its end column is C plus the
    column it returns when it has no newline, and that column alone when
    it has. "ab\nè😀" holds one newline and ends at column 2 in
    characters, 3 in UTF-16 and 6 in UTF-8. Measuring a long string this
    way costs one pass over it, far less than a loop in Lisp.

REPEATING
    C-x z repeats the last command, and pressing z again repeats it again.
    For anything longer, record what you did and play it back: see the
    `macros' page.

EXAMPLES
    Insert text from Lisp, several pieces at once:

        (insert "total: " 42 "\n")

    The line point is on, and its number:

        (list (line-number-at-point) (current-line))

    Do something at the end of the buffer and come back:

        (save-excursion
          (goto-char (point-max))
          (insert "\n;; end\n"))

    The first line of the buffer, leaving point where it is:

        (save-excursion (goto-char 0) (current-line))

    Pair brackets in C as well, but not quotes:

        (electric-pair-enable 'cpp-mode)
        (put 'cpp-mode 'electric-pair-inhibit-quotes t)

    Keep every closing bracket you type, even next to one already there:

        (setq electric-pair-skip-self nil)

    Stop drawing the bracket paired with the one at the cursor, in every
    buffer:

        (global-show-paren-mode -1)

    Leave the spaces at the ends of lines undrawn in one mode, where they
    mean something:

        (defun my-markdown-setup () (minor-mode-off 'whitespace-mode))
        (add-hook 'markdown-mode "after-change-major-mode-hook"
                  'my-markdown-setup)

    Clean every file of them as it is saved, but leave the blank lines at
    the end alone:

        (minor-mode-enable 'whitespace-cleanup-mode)
        (setq delete-trailing-lines nil)

    Clean only the Rust files:

        (minor-mode-enable 'whitespace-cleanup-mode 'rust-mode)

    Give the commands that have no key one of yours:

        (define-key nil "C-c d" 'duplicate-line)
        (define-key nil "C-c ;" 'comment-indent)

    What some edits changed, without copying the buffer:

        (track-changes-begin)
        (insert "abc")
        (track-changes-end)       ; (START 0 "abc" "")

    A watcher of your own, which vim's Insert sessions do not disturb:

        (track-changes-begin 'my-log)
        (delete-char 3)               ; with point before "abc"
        (track-changes-end 'my-log)   ; (START 3 "" "abc")

    Where point is, with the column in UTF-16 code units, and the way back
    to the same place:

        (let ((at (position-to-line-column (point) 'utf-16)))
          (goto-char (line-column-to-position (car at) (nth 1 at) 'utf-16)))

    Where text just deleted at point used to end, in UTF-8 bytes, from the
    text itself:

        (let ((at (position-to-line-column (point) 'utf-8))
              (size (string-line-column "one\ntwo" 'utf-8)))
          (if (= (car size) 0)
              (list (car at) (+ (nth 1 at) (nth 1 size)))
            (list (+ (car at) (car size)) (nth 1 size))))

    Rename a variable in two places and fix a number, with offsets found
    on the text as it is:

        (replace-regions '((4 5 "total") (20 21 "total") (12 13 "0")))

    Comment out a whole buffer from Lisp:

        (comment-region (point-min) (point-max))
